\section{Online Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate the savings available under PACE's cumulative budget, its comparison with published decision rules, and the contribution of each component.
We simulate task execution and compilation using measured cost profiles on synthetic streams and recorded arrival orders.

The evaluation uses two grids of $300$ conditions, each crossing three models, four verification settings, five arrival patterns, and five cost or environment settings, with eight paired runs per condition.
The second grid uses its own seeds and parameters.
We also use Sepsis and BPI 2019 process logs \citep{mannhardt2016sepsis,vandongen2019bpi} and two Wikipedia edit streams, giving twelve combinations of models and logs with ten paired runs.
The logs give task frequencies and arrival order, not live GUI tasks.
We shuffle measured cost profiles onto families and assign verification probabilities independently of initial compilation outcomes.
We estimate costs for successful or failed outcomes that were not observed and test how these estimates affect the results below.
The policies receive family labels, failure parameters, and an indication of detected GUI drift.
Appendix~\ref{app:simulation-details} specifies the grids, mappings, estimates for missing costs, and shared outcome sequences.

We compare PACE with ReAct \citep{yao2023react} and decision-rule adaptations of AutoRPA \citep{chen2026autorpa} and ToolPro \citep{liu2026toolpro} under the same compiler and cost model.
ReAct serves every arrival with the agent.
AutoRPA makes one compilation attempt when enough source traces are available and does not retry a rejected attempt.
ToolPro compares the compilation cost with the saving available from the current task, without projecting future reuse.
These adaptations differ only in when they call the same compiler.
All policies pay the same routing, extraction, fallback, and actual compilation charges, so differences in compiler quality do not affect this comparison of decision rules.
Each table ratio divides a policy's mean cost by PACE's mean cost in the same condition.
Overall results average these ratios with equal weights.
Reported percentage savings are the equally weighted means of $100(1-K_{\mathrm{PACE}}/K_{\mathrm{baseline}})$ across conditions, using each condition's mean costs.
We use $2000$ paired bootstrap resamples of runs, conditional on the tested conditions and given profiles.
We used these grids while developing PACE, so the comparisons are not independent confirmation.
The main tables give point estimates, with complete conditional intervals in Appendix~\ref{app:full-comparisons}.

\subsection{Savings}
\label{sec:savings}

Across the grids, ReAct costs $1.107$ and $1.130$ times PACE on average (Table~\ref{tab:policysim}).
Across $4800$ grid runs and $120$ log runs, every recorded cumulative cost ratio to the agent reference stays within the implemented $1.25$ bound.
This checks the implementation on those runs.
Theorem~\ref{thm:cost-bound} covers all histories allowed by the model.
AutoRPA's largest grid ratios to PACE are $19.314$ and $26.538$.
ToolPro matches ReAct cost in these conditions because its compilation rule counts savings from only one task.

On the recorded logs, PACE reduces token costs by $17.34\%$ relative to ReAct, with a conditional $95\%$ interval of $[14.96,19.61]\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:realstreams}).
It reduces cost in nine of twelve conditions.
Its largest cost ratio to ReAct is $1.030$, and its lowest is $0.578$.
Relative to PACE, AutoRPA and ToolPro have mean cost ratios of $1.362$ and $1.262$, respectively.
AutoRPA costs more in every condition, while ToolPro costs less in three.
The comparison concerns the stated decision-rule adaptations, rather than the original systems end to end.
Cost-aware proposals under the same budget have similar costs (Appendix~\ref{sec:matched-budget}).

Removing the savings test raises mean cost on the recorded logs by $52.68\%$ (conditional $95\%$ interval $[48.25,58.36]\%$).
Using the same estimated cost for successful and failed attempts raises it by $23.34\%$ ($[19.18,28.06]\%$).
The equal-cost variant raises mean cost for all three models.
Both variants keep the budget, so their largest grid ratios to ReAct remain near the same $1.25$ limit despite higher mean costs.
Removing the budget changes mean cost on the recorded logs by only $0.0013\%$, yet raises the largest tested grid ratio to ReAct from $1.2468$ to $1.7563$, or $40.9\%$.
Thus the near-zero effect on the recorded logs concerns average cost, while the budget's measured contribution is protection against larger losses.
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-absolute} gives the cost ratios and paired intervals, including conditions where an ablation is cheaper.

\paragraph{Cost assumptions.}
For missing costs, we vary the assumed ratio of failed to successful compilation costs over $0.5,1,2,5$.
This gives mean PACE-to-ReAct cost ratios from $0.833$ to $0.866$ on the recorded logs.
Assigning high compilation costs to frequent families with low verification probability raises the mean to $1.015$, about $1.5\%$ above ReAct, while preserving the cost constraint.
This assignment makes programs costly to produce for the families with the most reuse opportunities.
The resulting loss of savings shows why task frequency alone is not enough to decide when to compile.
The budget still limits extra spending, but it cannot create savings when the available compilation costs and useful lifetimes make reuse unprofitable.
