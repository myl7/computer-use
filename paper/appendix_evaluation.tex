\section{Online evaluation details}\label{app:simulation-details}

\subsection{Cost profiles}
\label{app:cost-profiles}

We construct cost profiles for the online evaluation from measurements of seven families for GLM and DeepSeek and six families with compilation records for Qwen.
The refused Qwen web request provides no cost profile.
For a cell with a successful compilation, we use its agent cost, extraction cost, and successful compilation cost.
We use its deployment failure fraction as $q$ conditional on no GUI drift.
Each test setting defines $h$ separately.
A cell without a successful compilation uses medians from cells with successful compilations under the same model for the unobserved successful-program quantities.
The agent success probability is the fraction of completed exploration attempts that succeeded, including unsuccessful attempts in the denominator.

\looseness=-1
A complete failed compilation gives the failed-attempt cost for that cell.
For an unobserved failed outcome, we use the same model's median complete failed-attempt cost.
For Web DeepSeek, whose recorded charge is incomplete, we set this estimate to at least its recorded cost lower bound.
We exclude incomplete charges from the donor median.
These values define fixed charges in the experiments.
The estimates of missing costs are assumptions about unobserved outcomes, not additional measurements.

\subsection{Synthetic task streams}
We evaluate two grids of $300$ conditions each.
Each includes all combinations of three model profiles, four verification settings, five arrival patterns, and five cost or environment settings.
The first grid has $1200$ arrivals and $30$ families that appear repeatedly, except in its sparse pattern.
Its verification probabilities are $0.9$, $0.5$, $0$, or an independently assigned per-family mixture over $\{0,0.1,0.5,0.9,1\}$.
Its patterns are uniform family sampling, Zipf sampling, bursts, a change in the frequent family, and infrequent repeats.
The uniform pattern concerns family labels, not wall-clock Poisson arrivals.
The base setting uses $h=0.02$, an idle limit of $T=100$, and router cost $368+91\ell_t$.
We measure routing cost with program lists of up to $100$ entries and use the resulting intercept and rounded per-entry cost.
We use these constants as shared scenario parameters across model profiles.
The other settings multiply only failed-compilation cost by five, multiply both compilation costs by five, set $h=0.1$, or raise the per-entry routing charge to $1820$.

\looseness=-1
The second grid uses its own random seeds and parameters.
It has $1800$ arrivals and $40$ families that appear repeatedly, except in its sparse pattern, with verification probabilities $0.95$, $0.35$, $0$, or a mixture over $\{0,0.05,0.3,0.7,1\}$.
Its altered-cost settings multiply failed cost by three or both compilation costs by seven, and its drift and routing settings use $h=0.07$ and $m=910$.
We report every condition in both grids.
The probabilities are assigned independently of the profiles' initial verification outcomes, so an estimate for a missing value does not identify a family's verification probability.

\subsection{Recorded arrival orders}\label{app:arrival-orders}
We also use Sepsis and BPI 2019 process logs \citep{mannhardt2016sepsis,vandongen2019bpi} and two Wikipedia edit streams.
In each process log, an arrival is a completed case, ordered by its earliest event, and a family is its full ordered activity sequence.
Sepsis contains $1050$ arrivals and $846$ families, while BPI 2019 contains $251{,}734$ arrivals and $11{,}973$ families.
These activity-sequence labels are assigned after each case completes and do not test online family identification.

\looseness=-1
For Wikipedia, an arrival is an edit, and families group normalized edit-summary templates with bot and tool labels.
The tool stream contains $65{,}564$ arrivals and $2529$ families.
We use the first $100{,}000$ timestamp-ordered human edits, which contain $68{,}773$ families.
Each human edit with an empty or section-only summary gets its own family label.
The source window spans 3 September 2026 at 18:48:26 UTC to 5 September 2026 at 18:48:26 UTC, as recorded in \path{datasets/wiki-stream/structure_report.md}.
The exact summary normalization is in \path{datasets/wiki-stream/build_families.py}, and \path{code/t2sim/streams.py} defines sorting and stream selection.

\looseness=-1
The logs give task frequencies and arrival order rather than live GUI tasks.
For each run, measured cost profiles are shuffled onto log-family identifiers, and verification probabilities are assigned independently from the first grid's mixture.
Three models and four logs give twelve combinations of models and logs.
The runs use the same base drift and routing settings as the first grid.

\paragraph{Confidence intervals.}
Each grid condition has eight paired runs, and each combination of a model and a log has ten.
Compilation outcomes are indexed by family and attempt, while serving outcomes are indexed by family, arrival, and failure channel.
Rules in a run share these outcome sequences and the same cost mapping.
A cell's cost ratio is the policy's mean token cost divided by the PACE mean in that cell.
We compute the overall result by averaging the cell ratios with equal weights, without combining absolute costs across models.
The grid and log intervals use $2000$ paired bootstrap resamples, with run indices shared across policies and conditions in each study.
They describe variation in the saved mappings and outcomes, conditional on the tested scenarios and given cost profiles.
They do not account for measurement error in the given costs or the effects of choosing different estimates of missing costs.
The largest tested condition mean is a descriptive maximum and has no confidence interval.

We used both grids while developing PACE, so the reported comparisons are exploratory evidence rather than independent confirmation.
All grid and log conditions use detected failures and one agent fallback.
The experimental environment reports whether a failure is due to GUI drift.
The policy uses this report to stop program reuse.
We do not test an online drift detector.
When a program fails, the model uses the same agent outcome as ReAct for that arrival.
This rule ensures that task success in the model cannot be lower than ReAct's.
The cost bound itself makes no such quality claim.

\subsection{Comparison rules}\label{app:baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

\looseness=-1
We compare PACE with ReAct, two decision strategies adapted from published systems, and alternative timing rules with and without the same cumulative budget.
All methods use the same measured compiler and serving profiles, so the comparison isolates their compilation and reuse decisions.
The named adaptations do not reproduce the original systems' models, code generators, or benchmark scores.

\paragraph{ReAct.}
The ReAct baseline uses agent execution on every arrival and never compiles or reuses programs \citep{yao2023react}.

\paragraph{AutoRPA (one build).}
AutoRPA constructs a program from source tasks, limits its repair attempts, and uses ReAct when construction fails \citep{chen2026autorpa}.
Our adaptation attempts one build after $k=3$ completed agent runs, with the measured compilation charge already including bounded repair.
A failed build leaves the family on the agent path.
If the build succeeds, the program serves later tasks with agent fallback, and detected GUI drift returns the family to agent service without another build.
This transfers the bounded construction-and-reuse policy to the shared compiler and arrival model.

\paragraph{ToolPro (cost adaptation).}
ToolPro compares construction latency with the latency saved within one task \citep{liu2026toolpro}.
We adapt this single-task criterion to token cost: a family with enough traces and no program to reuse requests compilation only when $s_h>\hat B$.
The estimate $\hat B$ uses the same observed verification outcomes as PACE.
This rule gives no credit for savings on multiple future tasks.
We keep the shared parameterized programs and serving order, so the comparison tests the cost criterion rather than ToolPro's original tool execution system.

\paragraph{Cost-aware timing controls.}
\looseness=-1
The remaining controls share a per-family budget that allows compilation only when $F+C^{\mathrm{fail}}\leq\max(0,S)+\max(0,\hat Hs_h)$, where $F$ is failed-compilation spend and $S$ is accumulated program-use saving.
The allowance uses estimated future savings and does not enforce PACE's cumulative bound.
\emph{Eager + allowance} requests a build when the family has enough traces and no program to reuse.
\emph{After 10 arrivals} waits for ten arrivals of the family.
\emph{After 10 successes} waits for ten successful agent runs since the last compilation attempt or detected GUI drift.
\emph{Savings threshold} waits until the expected savings lost by using the agent instead of reusing a program reach $\hat B$.
\emph{Estimate + allowance} uses PACE's savings test with this allowance in place of its cost budget.

\paragraph{The same budget with other proposals.}
\looseness=-1
The budgeted rows in Tables~\ref{tab:policysim-full} and~\ref{tab:realstreams-full} hold PACE's $0.25$ margin, service reservations, and program list controls fixed.
\emph{Eager + both checks}, \emph{Arrival-10 + both}, and \emph{Estimate + both} add that budget to the corresponding estimate-based allowance rules.
\emph{Count + budget} changes only PACE's estimated reuse count to $\min(N,1/h)$.
\emph{Optimistic + budget} keeps PACE's estimate $\hat H$ but uses the one-sided $90$ percent Clopper--Pearson upper value in place of $\hat p$, using it as an optimistic proposal estimate.
\emph{One initial try} adds a limit to that optimistic rule: an unsuccessful first build ends compilation for the family, while an initially successful family can later attempt replacement.
The budget guarantee does not require these proposal estimates to be accurate.

All methods require $k=3$ completed agent runs before compilation and serve the current arrival before attempting a build.
They share free program lookup, idle expiry, adding programs to the list again if they can still be reused, and the same failure outcomes.
PACE and the other budgeted rules check service and compilation costs, and can clear the program list before a routing charge.
PACE's main setting is $\epsilon=0.25$ throughout both comparison tables.
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} separately removes each of its main components.


\subsection{Cost-profile sensitivity}\label{app:cost-sensitivity}

The base profiles contain eleven observed successful compilation costs, eight complete failed-attempt costs, and one failed-attempt cost lower bound.
We vary the cost of the unobserved outcome by setting target ratios $r=C^{\mathrm{fail}}/C$ to $0.5$, $1$, $2$, or $5$, while keeping the cost of each observed outcome unchanged.
For a profile with an observed successful compilation, we vary $C^{\mathrm{fail}}$.
For a profile with a complete failed attempt, we vary $C$.
For Web DeepSeek, we keep the estimate of the missing $C$ and set the failed cost to the larger of its recorded lower bound and $rC$.
Its actual ratio therefore exceeds the target value in all four settings.
The other profile values and the base probability assignments remain fixed.
These four settings give forty-eight combinations of models and logs.

We add two dependence scenarios using the base costs.
Verification probability decreases from $0.9$ to $0.1$ as the profile's rank in $C/c$ increases.
One scenario keeps the base profile assignments.
The other assigns profiles with high $C/c$ to frequent families without changing which profiles are used or how many times each is assigned.
Only the code that creates the workload uses counts from the full stream.
The policy receives neither those counts nor the hidden verification probabilities.
We fixed these seventy-two conditions before evaluation and saved them all.
They test alternatives to the base assumptions rather than estimate how these factors vary together in an actual deployment.

We separately test the incomplete Web DeepSeek charge at its recorded lower bound and at twice the modeled estimate, keeping all other settings fixed.
We use the default bursty condition and the BPI 2019 condition, giving four additional conditions that are excluded from the main comparison averages.

Across the four missing-cost settings, PACE's mean cost ranges from $0.833$ to $0.866$ of ReAct.
The Equal attempt costs variant costs $1.020$ and $1.141$ times PACE on average at $r=2$ and $r=5$.
PACE costs more than this variant in six of twelve conditions at $r=0.5$ and in two at $r=2$, but less in all twelve at $r=5$.
At $r=1$, the eight GLM and Qwen conditions tie exactly.
For DeepSeek, the partial web spending record sets a minimum failed-attempt cost, so differences remain in the other four conditions.
The value of using separate costs therefore depends on the assumed difference between successful and failed compilation costs and how that difference changes compilation timing.

\looseness=-1
Under cost-dependent verification alone, PACE has a mean cost of $0.671$ times ReAct cost.
Adding the assignment of costly profiles to frequent families raises that ratio to $1.015$, so PACE costs about $1.5\%$ more than ReAct on average.
Equal attempt costs has a mean cost ratio to PACE of $1.056$ in the same joint scenario.
Both variants satisfy the implemented budget check, with a relative numerical tolerance of $10^{-8}$, in all $1440$ runs.
The largest recorded prefix ratio is $1.25000001$.
These results separate the cost constraint from the amount saved, which depends on the workload and given cost model.

In the separate Web DeepSeek missing-charge check, PACE-to-ReAct cost ratios range from $0.686$ to $0.785$ on the default bursty condition and from $0.545$ to $0.618$ on BPI 2019.
The corresponding ratios under the modeled failed cost are $0.734$ and $0.624$.
All four additional conditions satisfy the implemented budget.

\subsection{Alternative compilation proposals}
\label{sec:matched-budget}

Applying PACE's budget to the cost-aware timing controls preserves their low costs.
On the recorded logs, Eager + both checks, Arrival-10 + both, and Estimate + both have mean costs of $0.8349$, $0.8323$, and $0.8312$ times ReAct cost, compared with $0.8266$ for PACE.
Each paired $95$ percent interval for control cost divided by PACE cost lies above one, although each mean cost difference is below $1\%$.

Count + budget has mean cost ratios to ReAct of $0.9556$ and $0.9536$ on the two grids, compared with $0.9563$ and $0.9539$ for PACE.
On the recorded logs, its mean cost is $0.8265$ times ReAct cost.
Its paired $95\%$ interval for the cost ratio to PACE is $[0.9997,0.9999]$.
The count rule is slightly cheaper than PACE's estimate $\hat H$ in these conditions.
Optimistic + budget and One initial try have higher log means of $0.9203$ and $0.8839$.
Together, these results support cost-aware proposals within the shared cost budget, but do not identify the exact reuse-count estimate as a separate source of improvement.
