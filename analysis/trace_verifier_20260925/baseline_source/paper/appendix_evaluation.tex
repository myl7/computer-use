\section{Online evaluation details}\label{app:simulation-details}

\subsection{Cost profiles}
\label{sec:policysim}

We construct cost profiles for the online evaluation from measurements of seven families for GLM and DeepSeek and six retained families for Qwen.
The refused Qwen web request provides no cost profile.
A verified cell supplies its agent cost, extraction cost, program failure fraction, and successful compilation cost.
A cell without a verified program uses the same model's verified-cell medians for the unobserved successful-program quantities.
The agent success probability is the fraction of completed exploration attempts that succeeded, including unsuccessful attempts in the denominator.

A complete failed compilation supplies that cell's failed-attempt charge.
For cells without such a record, DeepSeek uses its median cost of completed failed compilation attempts and Qwen uses the cost of its one completed failed compilation attempt.
The partial Qwen Calc spend is used only for that cell and is excluded from the other failure-cost substitutions.
The initial GLM profiles have no complete failed attempt, so their base setting uses $C^{\mathrm{fail}}=C$.
These values define fixed charges in the experiments.
The missing-cost substitutions are assumptions about unobserved outcomes, not additional measurements.

\subsection{Synthetic task streams}
We evaluate two grids of $300$ conditions each.
Each crosses three model profiles, four verification settings, five arrival patterns, and five cost or environment settings.
The first grid has $1200$ arrivals and $30$ families that appear repeatedly, except in its sparse pattern.
Its verification probabilities are $0.9$, $0.5$, $0$, or an independently assigned per-family mixture over $\{0,0.1,0.5,0.9,1\}$.
Its patterns are uniform family sampling, Zipf sampling, bursts, a change in the frequent family, and infrequent repeats.
The uniform pattern concerns family labels, not wall-clock Poisson arrivals.
The base setting uses $h=0.02$, an idle limit of $T=100$, and router cost $368+91\ell_t$.
The other settings multiply only failed-compilation cost by five, multiply both compilation costs by five, set $h=0.1$, or raise the per-entry routing charge to $1820$.

The second grid changes both the random seeds and the parameters.
It has $1800$ arrivals and $40$ families that appear repeatedly, except in its sparse pattern, with verification probabilities $0.95$, $0.35$, $0$, or a mixture over $\{0,0.05,0.3,0.7,1\}$.
Its altered-cost settings multiply failed cost by three or both compilation costs by seven, and its drift and routing settings use $h=0.07$ and $m=910$.
We retain every condition in both grids.
The probabilities are assigned independently of the profiles' initial verification outcomes, so a missing-value substitution does not identify a family's verification probability.

\subsection{Recorded arrival orders}\label{app:arrival-orders}
We also use Sepsis and BPI 2019 process logs \citep{mannhardt2016sepsis,vandongen2019bpi} and two Wikipedia edit streams.
In each process log, an arrival is a completed case, ordered by its earliest event, and a family is its full ordered activity sequence.
Sepsis contains $1050$ arrivals and $846$ families, while BPI 2019 contains $251{,}734$ arrivals and $11{,}973$ families.
These activity-sequence labels are assigned after each case completes and do not test online family identification.

For Wikipedia, an arrival is an edit, and families group normalized edit-summary templates with bot and tool labels.
The tool stream contains $65{,}564$ arrivals and $2529$ families.
We use the first $100{,}000$ timestamp-ordered human edits, which contain $68{,}773$ families.
Human edits with empty or section-only summaries receive separate singleton labels.

The logs supply task repetitions and their order rather than live GUI tasks.
For each repetition, measured cost profiles are shuffled onto log-family identifiers, and verification probabilities are assigned independently from the first grid's mixture.
Three models and four logs give twelve combinations of models and logs.
The runs use the same base drift and routing settings as the first grid.

\paragraph{Confidence intervals.}
Each grid condition has eight paired repetitions, and each combination of a model and a log has ten.
Compilation outcomes are indexed by family and attempt, while serving outcomes are indexed by family, arrival, and failure channel.
Rules in a repetition share these outcome sequences and the same cost mapping.
A cell's cost ratio is the policy's mean token cost divided by the PACE mean in that cell.
An aggregate is the equally weighted mean of cell ratios, without pooling absolute cost units across models.
The grid and log intervals use $2000$ paired bootstrap resamples, with repetition indices shared across policies and conditions in each study.
They describe variation in the retained mappings and outcomes, conditional on the tested scenarios and supplied cost profiles.
They do not account for measurement error in the supplied costs or the effects of choosing different missing-cost substitutions or protocols.
The largest tested condition mean is a descriptive maximum and has no confidence interval.

The grids were run during protocol development, and the second grid changed settings after the first study.
The two literature adaptations and the equal-attempt-cost ablation were specified later and evaluated on these same conditions without changing PACE.
We preserve both grids, the alternative rules, the separate development cases, and the boundary cases in the result records.
The reported comparisons are exploratory evidence rather than independent confirmation of the final protocol choice.
All reported main conditions use detected failures and one agent fallback.
The experimental environment reports whether a failure is a persistent break, so invalidation uses a supplied outcome rather than a measured online classifier.
Because a failed program uses the same agent outcome as the ReAct baseline on that arrival, nondecreasing task success in this model follows from its construction.
The cost bound itself makes no such quality claim.

\subsection{Comparison rules}\label{app:baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

We compare PACE with ReAct, two decision strategies adapted from published systems, and alternative timing rules with and without the same cumulative budget.
All methods use the same measured compiler and serving profiles, so the comparison isolates their compilation and reuse decisions.
The named adaptations do not reproduce the original systems' models, code generators, or benchmark scores.

\paragraph{ReAct.}
The ReAct baseline uses agent execution on every arrival and never compiles or reuses programs \citep{yao2023react}.

\paragraph{AutoRPA (one build).}
AutoRPA constructs a program from building tasks, limits its repair attempts, and uses ReAct when construction fails \citep{chen2026autorpa}.
Our adaptation attempts one build after $k=3$ completed agent runs, with the measured compilation charge already including bounded repair.
A failed build leaves the family on the agent path.
A verified program serves later tasks with agent fallback, and a detected break returns the family to agent service without another build.
This transfers the bounded construction-and-reuse policy to the shared compiler and arrival model.

\paragraph{ToolPro (cost adaptation).}
ToolPro compares construction latency with the latency saved within one task \citep{liu2026toolpro}.
We adapt this single-task criterion to token cost: an eligible family requests compilation only when $s_h>\hat B$.
The estimate $\hat B$ uses the same observed verification outcomes as PACE.
This rule gives no credit for savings on multiple future tasks.
We retain the shared parameterized programs and serving order, so the comparison tests the cost criterion rather than ToolPro's original tool execution system.

\paragraph{Cost-aware timing controls.}
The remaining controls share a per-family allowance that admits compilation only when $F+C^{\mathrm{fail}}\leq\max(0,S)+\max(0,\hat Hs_h)$, where $F$ is failed-compilation spend and $S$ is accumulated program-use saving.
The allowance uses estimated future savings and does not enforce PACE's cumulative bound.
\emph{Eager + allowance} requests a build whenever the family is eligible and has no live program.
\emph{After 10 arrivals} waits for ten arrivals of the family.
\emph{After 10 successes} waits for ten successful agent runs since the last admission or detected break.
\emph{Savings threshold} waits until the forgone expected savings on agent execution without program reuse reach $\hat B$.
\emph{Estimate + allowance} uses PACE's estimated-savings test with this allowance in place of its cost budget.

\paragraph{The same budget with other proposals.}
The budgeted rows in Tables~\ref{tab:policysim-full} and~\ref{tab:realstreams-full} hold PACE's $0.25$ margin, service reservations, and manifest controls fixed.
\emph{Eager + both checks}, \emph{Arrival-10 + both}, and \emph{Estimate + both} add that budget to the corresponding estimate-based allowance rules.
\emph{Count + budget} changes only PACE's estimated future uses to $\min(N,1/h)$.
\emph{Optimistic + budget} retains its smoothed count but substitutes the one-sided $90$ percent Clopper--Pearson upper value for $\hat p$, using it as an optimistic proposal estimate.
\emph{One initial try} adds a limit to that optimistic rule: an unsuccessful first build ends compilation for the family, while an initially successful family can later attempt replacement.
The budget guarantee does not depend on the calibration of any of these proposal estimates.

All methods require $k=3$ completed agent runs before compilation and serve the current arrival before attempting a build.
They share free artifact lookup, idle expiry, relisting of live programs, and the same failure outcomes.
PACE and the other budgeted rules check service and compilation costs, and can clear the manifest before a routing charge.
PACE's main setting is $\epsilon=0.25$ throughout both comparison tables.
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} separately removes each of its main components.


\subsection{Cost-profile sensitivity}\label{app:cost-sensitivity}
\label{sec:price-sensitivity}

The base profiles contain fifteen observed successful compilation costs, four costs from completed failed compilation attempts, and one partial spend.
We test the unobserved counterpart by setting nominal ratios $r=C^{\mathrm{fail}}/C$ to $0.5$, $1$, $2$, or $5$, while preserving each observed branch cost.
For a verified profile we vary $C^{\mathrm{fail}}$, and for a profile with a complete failed attempt we vary $C$.
For Qwen Calc, we retain the imputed $C$ and set the failed cost to the larger of its partial spend and $rC$.
Its actual ratio therefore exceeds the nominal value in the first three settings.
The other profile values and the original probability assignments remain fixed.
These four settings give forty-eight combinations of models and logs.

We add two dependence scenarios using the original costs.
Verification probability decreases from $0.9$ to $0.1$ as the profile's rank in $C/c$ increases.
One scenario keeps the original profile assignments.
The other assigns profiles with high $C/c$ to frequent families while preserving the multiset of assigned profiles.
Only the environment constructor uses the full-stream counts, and the policy receives neither those counts nor the hidden verification probabilities.
We fixed these seventy-two conditions before evaluation and retained them all.
They test specified alternatives to the base assumptions rather than calibrating a deployment's joint distribution.

Across the four missing-cost settings, PACE's mean cost ranges from $0.788$ to $0.830$ of ReAct.
The Equal attempt costs variant costs $1.034$ and $1.143$ times PACE on average at $r=2$ and $r=5$.
The comparison also has losses: PACE costs more in four of twelve conditions at $r=0.5$ and in one at $r=5$.
At $r=1$, the eight GLM and DeepSeek conditions tie exactly, while the Qwen partial-spend floor leaves differences in the other four.
The value of separate costs therefore depends on the assigned asymmetry and its effect on compilation timing.

Under cost-dependent verification alone, PACE has a mean cost of $0.657$ times ReAct cost.
Adding the assignment of costly profiles to frequent families raises that ratio to $0.997$, so the base saving nearly disappears.
Equal attempt costs has a mean cost ratio of $1.050$ in the same joint scenario.
Both variants satisfy the implemented budget in all $1440$ protected runs.
These results separate the cost constraint from the amount saved, which depends on the workload and supplied cost model.

\subsection{Alternative compilation proposals}
\label{sec:matched-budget}

Applying PACE's budget to the cost-aware timing controls preserves their low costs.
On the recorded logs, Eager + both checks, Arrival-10 + both, and Estimate + both have mean costs of $0.7824$, $0.7809$, and $0.7817$ times ReAct cost, compared with $0.7815$ for PACE.
Each paired $95$ percent interval for control cost divided by PACE cost includes one.
These comparisons do not establish a cost advantage for PACE's proposal over the protected cost-aware controls.

Count + budget has mean cost ratios of $0.9090$ and $0.9066$ on the two grids, below PACE's $0.9095$ and $0.9070$.
Its log mean is $0.7819$, and its paired ratio to PACE has interval $[0.9999,1.0008]$.
The count rule has a similar cost to the smoothed estimate in these conditions.
Optimistic + budget and One initial try have higher log means of $0.8512$ and $0.8473$.
Together, these results support cost-aware proposals within the shared cost budget, but do not identify the exact use-count estimate as a separate source of improvement.

\subsection{Component costs}
\label{app:ablation-absolute}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation-absolute} gives cost ratios relative to PACE for the ablations, and Table~\ref{tab:ablation-effects-full} gives their paired percentage changes with intervals.
A change is the mean of $100(K_{\mathrm{variant}}/K_{\mathrm{PACE}}-1)$ over fixed combinations of models and conditions, using each condition's mean costs.
Effects can interact and are not additive shares of a total saving.
PACE's zero change is exact by construction.
On the recorded logs, equal attempt costs changes no GLM proposal because its base profiles already have $C^{\mathrm{fail}}=C$.
The mean log effect of removing the budget is small, while its worst grid cost increases.
By contrast, the savings-test and cost-estimate ablations retain the same budget, which keeps their maximum cost ratios to ReAct near the same limit.
Equal attempt costs is cheaper than PACE in $41$ and $48$ conditions of the two grids, so its average loss is not uniform across conditions.
